PETE’S TWALK OBSERVATIONS
Peter Squires, May 2002


TWALK is born

TWALK as we know it at CUTC is now a unique event, probably the only one in regular operation anywhere in the world. It started from Melbourne University and was brought to New Zealand by a postgraduate Electrical Engineering student, Greg Martin, in 1967. The Police were involved in the first event, as irate farmers who hadn’t been asked for the use of their land decided to “borrow” some of the TWALK organisers' vehicles. Since then, the landowners have always been asked!. The Australian TWALKs evolved to rogaining, where everyone chooses their own checkpoints, because the large numbers of Twalking participants had to play follow the leader. (The Melbourne Rogaining Association has over 1500 members, and runs several events a year). The availability of large areas of suitable landscape in Australia also suits Rogaines. The New Zealand countryside is more compact and steeper, and it is hard to get a large enough map without having to climb to 6000 ft. or cross a major river.

Other groups in New Zealand have had the occasional TWALK, conceived from our TWALK, but have not succeeded in continuing the event for more than a few years. At Canterbury we have a unique situation - a body of students who never tire of the TWALK concept because there is always a turnover of people, a large enough student base to be sure of having a sufficient percentage who are crazy enough to enjoy TWALKS, and a good range of suitable countryside within reach of Christchurch. Thus CUTC is a world-leader in the uniquely crazy concept that is TWALK. Long may we reign!


The early years.

As mentioned, the first TWALK was interesting. It was on the side of Mt. Thomas, which was completely overgrown with gorse and blackberry in those days. The odd access track went up the side of the hill in a few places and this is where the checkpoints were put - by motorbike. The Twalkers, of course, didn’t have motorbikes, and were trying to go cross-country between the checkpoints. This caused excessively slow progress, compounded by a southerly front which arrived at dusk. Very few people made it to the hash-house at the end of section 1 before Sunday morning. My team got there at 3.00 am. My strongest memory is of looking out over the lowlands below Mt. Thomas sometime about midnight, and seeing torches flashing all over the place, as Twalkers sought refuge in farmers’ sheds, haystacks, and trucks. One team spent the night in a chicken-coop, along with the chickens. The hash-house itself was little more than a tramper’s tent in a wet clearing. Based on Australian practice, everyone had to take their own tents as their own hash-houses. (It doesn’t rain as much or as coldly in Australia.)

In subsequent years things were much better. The farmers were approached beforehand, we had decent hash-houses, and attention was paid to vegetation. In those days footwear and torches were a problem. There was no such thing as a “running shoe” other than a ballet shoe with spikes. We all wore heavy leather tramping boots. My pair weighed 6 lbs, or 2.8 kilograms. Blisters were the order of the day. We had unofficial competitions to develop the best torches. I created a dual-headlamp with a 6 volt searchlight and a 3 volt general light, all powered by a 6 volt battery with a transistorised inverter driving the 3 volt bulb. It still works! even with germanium transistors. Another Electrical Engineering student, Peter Napier, was not to be outdone. He carried a motorbike battery in his pack, with wires to a 12v. headlamp. It was certainly very bright. Unfortunately his wiring wasn’t up to scratch, and in the middle of the night he smelt a burning smell. One of his team-mates discovered that his pack was on fire, so the pack, battery, acid, clothes, and food were all hastily dumped in a nearby stream. His light doesn’t still work.

In those years TWALK was made into a party as much as a Twalk. We had roaring bonfires, lots of food, (and even beer) and many Twalkers would do section 1 only and then have an all-night party. A lot of competitive teams succumbed to the suction of the hash-house, and stayed to join the party. Numbers peaked at nearly 200 people! Since then, TWALK has evolved only slightly. More people spend more time walking now. They also spend more time searching for checkpoints! And the ultimate challenge is to stay awake on the bus-ride home.


Those #@** Checkpoints!

Since TWALK began over 34 years ago, there have been many course organisers and just as many interpretations of how to specify a checkpoint. Because the winners one year set the course the next year, there should be some consistency, but there often hasn’t been because the winners might have left Christchurch. The founding concept of TWALK was that it should be a test of tramping navigational skills and fitness, on maps of the same standard and scale as trampers use, and requiring the ability to find a tramping-sized feature on the ground, eg. a hut, a stream junction, a peak etc. Checkpoints were originally fairly well defined, and in Australian Rogaines, (where TWALK started from) they now use 30cm. square dayglo flags which must be visible from 20 metres in all directions! Orienteers, too, use similarly sized flags, and are not allowed to hide them. Anyone who has tried Orienteering will know how hard those flags can be to find, even on a large-scale, well-detailed map in broad daylight! In TWALK we have a bit more licence, but things started to get out of hand about 24 years ago with a description “under signpost”, which meant that the signpost had to be pulled out of its hole in the ground!. This flexibility is one of the strengths of TWALK but also one of its weaknesses - finding checkpoints can be a matter of luck more than skill, and this can negate the many other excellent challenges of TWALK, and also introduce a risk of hypothermia as sustained movement ceases while random searches are conducted. From my observations over many, many TWALKS (he said as his beard flashed silver in the moonlight and the last of his hair fell to the ground) I believe that the best compromise requires consistency from year to year, yet allows some imagination on the part of the planners. The guiding rule must be that a team who gets to the right spot within the accuracy of the map should be capable of finding the checkpoint without having to resort to any kind of random or large-area search. I have therefore composed the following suggestions for checkpoints which may or may not be adhered to by anyone now or in the future at any time, but will provide guidance for anyone who isn’t too sure about how well you should hide a checkpoint!


Checkpoint Guidelines

Each checkpoint has a grid reference which pinpoints an area to within 50 metres. This therefore defines an area 100 metres by 100 metres on the map. The description given for the checkpoint must be sufficiently detailed to define a unique point within the 100 metre square.

There should therefore be only one such feature within the square. If there is more than one, then the correct one must be defined, eg. "Matagouri 30 metres Northwest of hilltop". An alternative way might be to describe something unique about the object, eg. "tallest Cabbage tree", or "not a pine tree" if all trees except the right one are pine trees. "Middle tree" or "end tree" are suitable descriptions if there is a small group of trees in a regular shape, such as a shelter belt. Fenceposts must be uniquely defined; "third from intersection" or "western gatepost" are OK, but "fencepost" alone is inadequate.

If a landscape feature more prominent than the checkpoint exists in the 100 metre square, such as a stream junction or a hilltop, then reference should be made to this feature, eg "below junction", "southern side of valley", or "southwest of hilltop".

A 100 metre square can cover a lot of vertical height, and at night-time, it is virtually impossible to see over this distance. Hilly checkpoints which are not on natural tracks such as valleys or major ridges need extra description to put the searchers at the right altitude, and on the right side of features such as ridges, hilltops, or valleys. An example could be “Foot of rock bluff 1/3rd. way up eastern side of valley.”

The checkpoints themselves should preferably be visible to a searcher without having to disturb any ground, vegetation, rock, wood, or object. Any "hidden" checkpoints that require searchers to re-hide it for following teams may be over-enthusiastically hidden and should be avoided. If the checkpoint must be hidden from direct view the clue must indicate this, eg. “Under dead tree” or “At base of post” if there is long grass. Sometimes, the location of the checkpoint is so well defined that it won’t matter if it is a bit hidden. eg. “junction of three fences” or “trig station”. In such cases, the Twalkers must know with certainty that they are in the unique location where the checkpoint is hidden.

The cryptic description of the checkpoints is another endearing and unique feature of TWALK, and adds much to the challenge and enjoyment. Care must be taken that the encryption process is not too extreme. Perhaps a good guideline is that the clues should ensure that the Twalkers are at least in the right position without fully understanding the clue, and are capable of finding the checkpoint with a bit of effort. Sometimes the addition of a less-cryptic comment may be necessary. The checkpoint descriptions should be printed in sufficient quantities to ensure that each person has their own description, for each section. This enables more people in a big team to be involved with hunting for checkpoints.

TWALK forever,

Pete Squires.
