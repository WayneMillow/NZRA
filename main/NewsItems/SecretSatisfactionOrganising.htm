Secret Satisfactions of a Rogaine Organiser

Grant Hunter
July 2012

 

While most rogainers just wanna run, some find adventure in making events happen. I’ll argue for (I say) the deeper satisfactions of an organiser and planner. It’s in the satisfaction of the personal discovery of rural and natural landscapes that few other outsiders know about, let alone visit, but which we know rogainers will appreciate on the day. As much as possible with each event someplace new, we’re always on the scout.

It’s in the satisfaction of understanding landscapes. Course setters, like teams – but in advance of them – must read the nuances of physical landscape and the paper map, and in particular the spatial, shape and scale relationships linking them.

It’s in the aesthetic satisfaction of using an off-the-shelf topographical map to set a navigational challenge. The map (unlike say an orienteering map) is general purpose, from a set that covers the whole country, and scaling-up even further, part of a global system of maps. Within this vast context we can pin-point choice and often mini natural features ...a waterfall, a rock outcrop or patch of forest, …or a crooked gate, or derelict building, and turn them into something new.

It’s in the creative satisfaction of laying out the feature points on the map as a single event field that will challenge newcomers – but not overtax them – as well as world champions. Our world champions, by the way.

We’ll make these features as aesthetic and rewarding as we can for the satisfaction of teams, but my gosh we’ll make them work hard for it in body and mind.

Organisation is no desktop exercise. We’ll have visited every check point at least three times before we’re done, often four. This will be in our own time, with no tyranny of the timepiece frowning over our shoulders. (Our measure is quality of course, not points scored!) So, it’s in the physical satisfaction of our own exertions as well.

It’s in the professional satisfaction (for I work in sustainable land management) of overlaying our activity on how others – the long-term occupiers – work this land. We work cohesively with farmers and foresters: few turn us away. Several of our host landowners are by now regular participants – the wheel turns full circle. (This has become one of those neat little life satisfactions for me). Mostly, we’re judged as worthy associates in their landscapes. Interestingly, we’ve been a boundary-pushing group for conservation managers (do we stick to tracks, are there too many of us, do we leave muddy footprints, are we ‘commercial’?) Again, we work to build those relationships.

It’s in the satisfaction of engaging with rural host communities. To return the favour of access granted – more often than not we’re on private land, not public – we’ll offer local fundraising opportunities, such as catering. They appreciate these ‘quality dollars’ – risk-free for them as we guarantee the numbers, and ‘fresh’ dollars sourced from outside their own ranks rather than recirculated within.

And it’s in the satisfaction of our efforts appreciated by teams – of seeing teams return after 6 hours, or 12, or 24 of their own discovery, on their feet and on the move. Almost infallibly they wear tired smiles back to base. We’ll hear “thank you” at the finish way more often than we’d hear in a month of ordinary Sundays.

But it’s more than these parts one-by-one. For me the value of contemplatively cruising a new landscape to design an event that will test people’s sense of adventure, while bringing rural and urban/recreational communities closer together, measures up strongly against the value of simply taking part. Be there.

GGH,
July 2012
