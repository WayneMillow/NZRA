The Roxburgh Ramble 24 hr Rogaine
Planner's Observations   (Pete Squires)
28th December 2002


Well, this area really lived up to my expectations. After five days I have visited and marked 72 checkpoint sites, I have blisters on both heels, and I have sleep deprivation. I also have memories of many special places, and the generosity and assistance of so many landowners.

The area is surprisingly open and free of scrub and impenetrable gullies. In fact, it is so open that a number of the checkpoints will have to hang from stakes, as there is nothing else to tie them to. For the first time in a NZ 24-hour event, it is possible to travel almost at will in any direction. The only exceptions are a few out-of-bound areas and a rugged little stream running northwards through half of the map. The stream does have a selection of suitable crossing-points at convenient locations.

Although the altitude ranges from 80 to 1000 metres, most of the area is above 300 metres and there is a surprising amount of relatively flat travel available to those who choose their routes appropriately, with the start-finish area already at 600 metres.

The map is A3, vertically oriented to magnetic north (22 degrees east of true north) and is based on the standard 1:50,000 topographical series, with 20m contours. Grid lines 1 km apart show true North and East. A few magnetic grid lines will be marked on the map. Checkpoints are scattered over most of the map.

The north-eastern half of the map is in open tussock grassland cover, above 700 metres, and exposed to winds from any direction with little natural shelter available. People going there should carry heavy waterproof parkas and a thick thermal top and hat in addition to their normal polypropylene layers. Windproof trousers should also be carried. If the weather packs up, you will have to walk into a headwind for several hours to get home, and sleet is a distinct possibility. (The lake at the northern end was known as "Dismal Swamp" before it was dammed to form a lake.) On the other hand, there is good travel, relatively less climb, vast scenery, and a sense of isolation in this area. The tussock sometimes causes slower travel because of its density, but most of the area is still fairly easily and quickly negotiated.

The other half of the map is largely on well-developed hill country grassland, with some interesting gullies and streams. This area is lower in altitude and there are a number of shelterbelts and forestry plantations. Although some of the gentler ridges have been sown in fodder crops and are therefore out-of-bounds, good alternative travel is available around their edges (beside fences, or in gullies) or by making different route choices. This is generally a very pretty area.

I was disturbed to come across a number of deer fences (2m high netting fences) in my travels. Normally we regard deer farms as out-of-bounds. However in most cases these fences do not have deer anywhere near them; they are the standard method of replacing old fences. We have permission to cross them, either by crawling under them at a low point, or by finding a gate and going through. This is the best option and guess what - there are gates at all the most important places! We must NOT climb over the deer fences. Note though, that if the deer fence is the boundary of an out-of-bounds area, it is NOT to be crossed.

There is a surprising amount of natural water on the course, of excellent quality. Particularly in the tussock country, it is easy to replenish water bottles. Watersheds of only a few hundred metres length will usually have a small stream flowing at the bottom. Of course, this means that the probability of wet feet is high, although with dexterity and skill you may be able to ward off the evil moment. There will be a couple of waterdrops in the southern half of the map and four drops in total.

There are a few roads through the competition area but these will be of limited assistance. Some tracks in the north-eastern corner could give some assistance in the tussock.

Because of the openness of the country, the absence of trees, and the presence of cattle (not wild) it has been necessary to attach some of the checkpoints to bushes on the sides of small cliff-faces in streams so they are out of reach of the cattle. This means that they are not visible from above, and must be found from the stream bed or opposite bank.

Overall, this is a delightful area for a Rogaine (see the pictures), and the course Vetter, Frank Dennis, should have a number of interesting outings over the next five weeks.

Pete Squires.
