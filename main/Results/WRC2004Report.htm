Sixth World Rogaining Championships
Big Lake, Arizona, USA
  8th & 9th May 2004

Through the eyes of Pete Squires.

This was a unique event. Beautiful views, fast underfoot, accurate map, superb organization, brilliant waterstops, good weather, mix of open and forest, and easy gradients. However the most unusual feature was the elevation. The hash-house, and most of the course, was 9120 ft (2700 metres) above sea level. This meant that temperatures were very acceptable, ranging from +16 to -3°C over the duration of the event. Being past the full moon and close-ish to the equator meant 5 hours of pitch blackness which sounds horrible but wasn’t too bad.

The terrain was most pleasant and picturesque, with about 40% open alpine grassland and the balance easily-traversed and relatively sparse forest. Underfoot conditions were very comfortable for the majority if not the entire course, making it excellent for the "runners". There were few steep climbs and no sustained ones, which is probably just as well considering the altitude.

The map scale was altered from 1:50,000 to 1:40,000 to aid the unsighted (us) with 20 foot (6.2 metre) contours. The south-west part of the extensive map (three overlapping sheets of 30 cm x 45 cm) showed a few deep canyons (a mere 100 metres deep) with beautiful, clear blue streams meandering through the meadows at the canyon floors. Map detail was excellent, showing forest boundaries and the many tracks with remarkable and reliable accuracy. The initial impression of difficult navigation because of few contours was quickly allayed by the abundance of catching features and attack points. Although there was a full 5 hours of total darkness before the moon rise at 12:15 am, the general feeling was that the navigational difficulty was rather light for a championship event. Certainly, faster than expected travel was experienced by most teams.

There were 64 checkpoints ranging from 30 to 90 points, with an average value of about 63. The course had been so well worked out that there were no obvious "best routes", and in fact no good groupings of high value points. Course planning didn't have any obvious strategic requirements, and one could "connect the dots" in many different ways with similar effectiveness.

As a whole, the event was extremely well planned and administered in delightful and gentle country. The only downside was the presence of mosquitos which were neither anticipated nor advertised. Apparently caused by a late snowfall and thaw, they lay in wait at many of the bush checkpoints. Fortunately, unlike the Kamloops event (1998 World Championships), they were restricted in numbers and went away at night. However they had an effective bite!

The few Kiwis in the event performed well, especially in view of the now strong international field.
  • Greg Barbour, with Australian Dave Rowlands, yielded their previous championship status to two Americans, Mike Kloser and Michael Tobin, but still came second only 10 points behind with 3410 points, and won the Veteran's section.
  • Ted Van Geldermalsen will be known to many orienteers. Although entered as an Australian, we will claim him as a Kiwi. His team finished 4th overall and 2nd Veterans with 3190 points.
  • Our team (me, Bill and Anne Kennedy) finished 17th, and placed 1st X Supervets, 2nd X Vets, and 5th X Open with 2700 points. Our placings are almost identical to the last world champs.
  • Tony Gazley and Michael Wood came second in the Mens Supervets with 2320 points, and
  • the ever-enthusiastic team of Annie Sanderson and Nick Collins came 9th in the Mixed Vets class of 31 teams (1930 points).

Unfortunately, the team of Dave Laurie and Andy Buchanan struck trouble. Andy was suffering from a severe virus (I know what it was like because I had it 2 weeks earlier) and although he started well for the first 10 hours, the bot caught up and he became so ill that Dave had to wrap him up in all their spare clothing and a space blanket, while he ran 16 kms back to the hash-house for help. We met Dave on the road, after he had cadged a map from another team having left in such a rush that he forgot his own map. We worked our way to Andy (via a couple of conveniently placed checkpoints) and confirmed that he was warm and as comfortable as he could be. What a shame to come so far only to be beaten by uncontrollable events, but what a brave effort to start anyway.

For ourselves, I think we had a great advantage by spending nearly two weeks in an acclimatization plan (Bill's Boot Kamp I called it) involving exercise and living at elevations from 7000 to 9000 feet. We were 4 hours ahead of our plan by 8:00 pm, due to the fast nature of the country, and had to add extra during the night. We came out of the event feeling worn out but in good condition, (or so I thought) having covered 85 straight-line map kilometers. Two days later we did a 16km, 4000 foot round trip just north of LA in 4 hours just to work up an appetite. It was only after this walk that Anne commented that she was going to treat a blister that had arisen on top of one of the blisters that she already had from the event. That was the first we had heard about any foot troubles. Anne must be the toughest woman West or East of the International Date Line! She continues to be our "secret weapon".

Full results are available at http://rogaine.tucsonorienteering.org/.

Pete Squires.
