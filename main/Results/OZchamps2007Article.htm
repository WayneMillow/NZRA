A Rough Rogaine in the Red (Hot) Centre
Australian Championships
Eastern MacDonnell Ranges, NT, Australia
  Saturday 28th & Sunday 29th July 2007

Richard Robinson and Vivienne Prince (1st XV)


The 2007 Australian Rogaining Championships were a much anticipated event being the first ever ARC in the Northern Territory and set in the MacDonnell ranges 90km east of Alice Springs. I was teamed up with usual partner, Viv Prince from Christchurch.

We were both concerned about the weather, the sub-zero night time temperatures and the warm sunny days. I packed the warmest gear I had, not particularly for the race, but to avoid freezing the nights before and after. As it turned out, the weather was an issue, but not the one envisaged, with max and min both 8 - 10 °C above normal leading to daytime nudging 30 °C and nights barely below 10 °C.

 

The drive in showed the area to be as spectacular as the build-up and the brochures; red rocky ranges, gorges and creeklines with abundant amazing rock features. Wandering the 500 metres from my accommodation to the Hash I noted a marker in a gully high on the range adjacent to the Hash “might leave that one for the way back in” I thought. The pre-event data warned of Spinifex capable of piercing 1” steel plate and spiky acacia that was impenetrable. None was noted around the Hash, but I guess that didn’t mean much. It also noted dangerous loose scree on many slopes, and this was very evident. Finally, the info noted that the area east of the Hash was based on recent photogrametry while that to the west was based on old mapping. It was said not to impact navigability, something of a white lie with hindsight!

The groundsheet sized map was an Eric Andrews created work of art, but the course design appeared to have used it well. Very few checkpoints involved serious climb, and most that did were adjacent to saddles that you probably wanted to traverse to get to the next part of the course in any case. Also the average distance between checkpoints was close to 2km. Oh, and the marker behind the Hash was notable for having no circle associated with it! We struggled for a while determining reasonable planning km rates. All the pre-event data suggested hard and slow, but the map suggested a lot less climb than we had expected. Eventually we settled on km/hr rates of 4, 3, 2½ & 3½ for each of the 6 hour periods with 78km in total.



 

Interestingly, the course plan leapt out at us quite quickly with a loop through the flatter east and south east, that still had some seriously technical bits, in the afternoon then through the south, south-west and into the north west overnight with the rest of the north west and middle of the map in the morning. It gave us a great track run home, some good short-cut options and some healthy extra loops from daylight on. It also was designed to allow many, but far from all, of the legs in technical areas to be negotiated through creeklines as the ridges looked incredibly confused.

We headed off with a huge pack to the first checkpoint. We choose to walk as it was already quite hot and after Viv’s near death in the heat at WRC we wanted to be conservative. Our first checkpoint was a side gully off a main valley. Many did it as a big out and back and headed south. Fewer of us headed east and suddenly we were alone. Next one was a spur. We approached from the top and as we came towards it saw Jon Sutcliffe (running with 10 times ARC Champ David Rowlands) coming up to the head of spur and round onto the next one. Funny we think, must be too steep to get off the side. At the top we looked and saw the marker on that next spur so followed them. Glad we weren’t here at night! Our next was a gully just off a saddle in a low range. As we got to the bottom of the gully there’s Jon and David at the top, but no marker! Mmmmm, shades of Hells Hollow. We did the obligatory perimeter and cobweb search, without success, and headed off.

Much to our relief the next marker was on the nominated feature, then one more and water which involved crossing this amazing rock wall up to 10 metres high and several hundred metres long with strange gaps and holes in it. Sensational country! And it was bloody hot! Two more on the flat, including one very dodgy site which was in the right place but not easy to attack, and then into the technical hills. First in the hills was a narrow spur with big cliffs down either side and spectacular views. We then straight-lined a complex leg which was physical but gave assured navigation. A difficult ridge traverse then one of the most technically complex rogaining legs I’ve ever had. We nailed it, got back onto the plains and relaxed again for a while. More water then pushed on. We were five hours in, about 15 minutes up on schedule and feeling good.

Then a range crossing with a 110 pointer near the saddle, great views, good points and worth the pain. At dark we were about 20 minutes up. Second dark leg was across incredibly technical terrain, or one could dive back down to the valley and head back up a creek. We opted for the latter albeit Viv complained about the challenge of eating baked beans from a zip lok bag whilst scrambling down rockfaces. I said she should be thrilled I would take her out to such an exotic location for dinner, and even offered some creamed rice (from a tube) for dessert!

 

We cruised on, staying our 20 minutes ahead and just eating it up. Then, about 10:30pm we arrive at the designated point and no marker. It was a spur, we checked up and down and around. We were absolutely in the right place. After the early escapades we just went on but, as it turned out, the concentration was broken. Also, Viv’s feet were toasted. The heavy rock and hard country had taken a toll. We stopped for 15 minutes and dressed the blisters but they looked nasty and she did a “cat on a hot tin roof” impression for the rest of the event. Suddenly we were marginally behind schedule.

Got the next checkpoint with minor hesitation, but a strenuous climb into the following one and stuff on the ground that had almost no relation to the map lost us 20+ minutes. We headed out into a flat area, the sort we had been eating up, but here the vegetation was over head-high and the minor features we were relying on were nowhere to be seen. After an age we realised we had to be past it, and probably close to off the map. We headed back and started to find wandering rogainers. Then a brainwave, go to the other side of the main watercourse where the relief is substantial and things are obvious! Five minutes later we grab it, albeit the “spur” was more obvious by the marker on it than by the topography.

We next had to follow a major watercourse through a huge conglomeration of ranges and gorges. Would have been sensational in daylight, was spectacular enough in the dark! A 40-pointer three (10 metre) contours above the creek involved a 70-metre climb? Headed back onto the Ross River floodplain and ground our way across the 200 metre wide sandy river bed. A watercourse junction that involved one that appeared not to have flowed in the past century and another that disappeared 200 metres from the “junction” was eventually found by re-section from the nearest gorge in a range.

From the beginning, I had been fixated with checkpoint 81, which was an up and back climb on a big range. A 40-pointer 1½ km away turned it into 120 points. Viv was less convinced and after the “junction” suggested a short-cut bypassing both. I was having none of it and so we slogged back across the Ross River and, after a false start up to the 40-pointer in the cave (literally) we headed on towards 81. But after five minutes scaling cliffs in (close to) impenetrable scunge I conceded she was right and we had just wasted an hour of our lives, and our race, and turned back to the north.

Picked up water again after 9+ hours since the last time, grabbed an easy one in a saddle and then crossed a 200+ metre high range that was five 10-metre contours on the map. This led us onto a heavily vegetated plain with a checkpoint that could easily have been described as “random flag in open area near heavy scrub”. Actually, it was the checkpoint description (End of the spur – 80m NNW) that eventually led us to the marker as it seemed to be rather a long way from the centre of the circle. Suspect it would have been a lot easier in the daylight. Of course, taking a bearing out of that site led us to very strange places just as the dawn was breaking on the beautiful red cliffs. We got into the next valley over and tried to relocate, but it all looked strange. I climbed to the top of a 25-metre high hill and it started to make some sense, except that the hill I was on wasn’t on the map! We moved on and the sense did work and then we got into another area that was just white space on the map, but massive detail on the ground. Fortunately we were heading towards a major watercourse, but those who did the leg in reverse really struggled!



 

Another mistake, just tired perhaps, and the 10 minutes lost made us realise we needed to take drastic action. We had one range still to cross, but is was very complex and confused. The Spinifex to that time had been reasonable, albeit post-midnight it had seemed to get thicker. Now it became wall to wall, and we were over 2 hours behind schedule. The combination of very steep rocky climbs (and descents) on loose scree, with Spinifex penetrating the skin at every stride started to reduce the humour of the team! We grabbed an unplanned checkpoint in seriously steep country, having dropped a loop of four from our plan and then trekked across a 1km Spinifex and mongrel scrub infested plain to the track that would take us home.

As with all rogaine homeward trudges it took a long time and as with everything on this side of the map there was a lot of stuff out there that wasn’t shown. We picked up three checkpoints on the way in, including one on a watercourse that the map showed as running the other way and another on this amazing rock wall, then ground our way into the Hash. Our straight line total was just over 70km, with all the loss in the second half.

Our feet were trashed by the rock and hard ground, Viv’s especially, and we had made three hours of errors, almost all since midnight. We comforted ourselves that everyone had the same problems, and that any team that had gone into the north west first would probably retired to bed by midnight in disgust. And thus it proved to be. Our 2,300 points (actually 2,370 if the one missing but found 200 metres out of position by some others was credited) still gave us 9th (8th if you add the 70) overall and a very clear win in XV. It was a repeat of many previous events with a good plan poorly executed still prevailing over poorer plans probably well executed.

As an event the organisation was excellent. The detail on the admin side left nothing to chance, and the permanent trophies for individuals who won categories were superb pieces of rock (each from a specific named checkpoint site) lacquered and mounted on a wooden base. Also, the Hash, with unlimited camping, a bunkhouse, massive showers and a resort 500 metres away was a fantastic choice. My only issue was the misplaced checkpoints, something an ARC can ill-afford, the inconsistency of the west half of the map and the very steep Spinifex area that was unpleasant, potentially hazardous, and not required anyway. Still, overall a fantastic event and thoroughly appreciated by all who took the time to attend.

Richard Robinson
