Australian Rogaining Championships
“Over the Border”, Queensland
  21st & 22nd May 2005

Pete Squires.


This event was a delight. The country had plenty of variety, from fast, open plains in the North to steepish, bush-clad hills in the South. The open country had the odd eucalypt pockets, which gave shelter and shade but disguised the vital contours. Most, (but not all) of the country was kind on the feet, with short or sparse grass cover over smooth soil. We were warned about “jumping cactus” but didn’t see any. It is the first rogaine I have ever completed with dry feet all the way. That’s right; not even dew on the grass in the morning.

And yet the temperature was not hot, as you might think in Queensland. The air was cool with a gentle breeze from the South, and the sun was warm. Even mid-afternoon temperatures were comfortable, and the breeze prevented frost forming during the night, even though it got cold enough to warrant over-trousers.

Don’t be deluded; it was not an easy event. Although the height variation was modest by Kiwi standards, the navigational demand was high. The map was 1:50,000 with 10 metre contours, which means it looks twice as steep as it really is. When it doesn’t look steep, the navigation is almost impossible by comparison with NZ maps; particularly given the moon set at 3:00 am, the regular distribution of open trees, and the removal from the map of all fences, tracks, and minor roads.

There were 172 teams spread over three concurrent events; a six-hour (44 teams), a twenty-four hour (99 teams including us), and a roving 15-in-23 (29 teams), which means the finish time is 23 hours after the start time but you are only allowed on the course for 15 hours within that time. This is an interesting concept, as it means everyone gets some sleep but also a bit of darkness.

We planned 2700 points over 69 kms, heading southwards initially to get through the steeper, rougher country first. We figured that the country was not sufficiently steep to unduly tire Kiwis, used to hill training, and this proved to be the case. We thought the more open country would suit the reduced visibility at night time. This is not always a good philosophy, as the steeper contours in the hills often assist night-time navigation. In this case, though, we thought that the reduction in visibility caused by trees outweighed the advantage of obvious contours. In retrospect, there was no clear advantage either way.

Initially we made rapid progress over the flat, open land, but this slowed at the hillier and more heavily forested country. Nevertheless, we maintained a 45 minute gain over our schedule until shortly before dark, when the scrub intensified and the light faded rapidly. We held our time gain for a couple of hours until we went straight past a checkpoint, shaded from the moon by the tree it was in. We spent half an hour over-shooting, finding a definite point in the local stream, walking past the checkpoint again to find the open area above it, then retracing our steps until we tripped over it.

Soon after we saw a bright light just ahead of us and thought “here’s someone who can confirm we are heading the right way”, but no, it was a couple of Maori hunters spotlighting for roos. We asked them if the track they were on (unmarked on our maps) continued to the road at the next farmhouse. They asked to see our maps but made the startled observation “there are no damn roads on the map” so they had no idea where we (or they) were. Fortunately our own interpretation of the contours prevailed and we moved steadily on to the next disaster.

This occurred in an unusually rough part of the course, steep and rocky and riddled with Lantana, a scrubby bush a bit like a cross between matagouri and bushlawyer. Few navigational cues meant we had to travel through the thick stuff to stay in touch. I slipped on the side of a big boulder and descended until a lantana bush saved? me, and received a large graze on my thigh. Anne walked into a hidden stick which punctured her lower leg and required a trip to the hospital after the event. But we persevered, and crossed the border back into Queensland shortly after midnight.

The flatter contours here, combined with some irregular mapping of same, and a falling moon, all lead to confusion. We took 90 minutes to get one checkpoint. Depressed, we headed west to a major road and walked northward zombie-like until we found a nice log to sit on and “admire the map”. A glow in the eastern sky told me that my watch was well over an hour slow, because a twig had pulled out the crown, stopping it. Panic.

With the daylight, the knowledge that we had over 20 kms to cover, and the easy country we were now in, we made good time. By 9:30 we were almost back on schedule, having abandoned only one checkpoint to save time. We rounded a bend on a sandy track over some rocks when I sensed some movement at my feet. Instinctively I jumped in the air, throwing my legs sideways as I looked down. I saw a black snake striking at my legs but missing as it ran out of reach. I don’t remember seeing anything specific before this. We watched fascinated as the snake rapidly worked its way between Anne and me, less than two metres apart, and down into its hole in the rock. It was nearly one and a half metres long and moved with surprising grace. We considered ourselves fortunate that we hadn’t been bitten, and privileged to see such a magnificent creature so closely in the wild. We later identified it as a Taipan; shy and non-aggressive but with a deadly venom.

The rest of the morning was uneventful but very enjoyable, as we knew the end was in sight and the walking conditions were most pleasant. We finished 15 minutes before the noon deadline with a score of 2650 points; sufficient to win the Mixed Veterans and the Mixed Super Veterans categories, and place us third in the Mixed Open, and 20th overall.

There were 6 Kiwi teams in the 24-hour, plus Ted Van Geldermalsen (now in Aussie), and another two Kiwi teams in the 6 hour. All Kiwi teams did well, finishing in the top half.

  • We were 1st XV 1st SXV, and 3 XO, but we were beaten by Alan Holdaway and Mondo Kopua (curses curses) who scored 10 points more than us (curses curses).
  • Henry Beex, Mark Struthers, and Dennis Litt were 10th MV on 2600 points.
  • Mark Minehan and Rachel Barton were 9th XO with 2100 points.
  • The reliable team of Nick Collins and Annie Sanderson were 3rd XV on 2080 points.
  • Suzanne Scott and Robert Newbrook were 5th XV on 1930 points.

Meanwhile, Ted Van Geldermalsen with Derek Morris finished 4th overall on 3520 points, while the top score was David Rowlands and Mike Hotchkis on a remarkable 4000 points.

In the 6-hour, Annette Windross and Jan Litt placed 3rd WV, whole John and Trudi McKinnel placed 4 XO.

It was great to see such a good turnout from NZ, and it was great to enjoy such a good event.
