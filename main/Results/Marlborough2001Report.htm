Commentary by Susan.

You will all be well recovered from your exertions in the Medway Valley by the time you read this, and I hope that you have been studying the map, thinking about what you did, what you didn’t do, and perhaps what you should have done! Here are some of my thoughts.
Planning beforehand. A well laid out rogaining map should never offer an obvious answer to “what is the best route?”, whatever your ambitions as a competitor. So…

  • Did you notice that the points were evenly distributed over the map? Each quarter of the map offered the same number of points.
  • Did you notice that the three 100 point controls all required careful navigation (they were all “red” controls in orienteering terms), and all three 90 pointers were easy to find (they were all “yellow” in orienteering terms). This means that the 100s would be very hard to find in the dark, and the 90s would all be relatively easy. Did you think about that?
  • Did you measure the control circles and note that they are 6mm = 300m across? The control is in the centre, so it is 150m from the edge of the circle (scale 1:50,000  means 100m on the ground = 2mm on the map).The circle edge can be a distance guide as you approach the control.
  • Did you look for clear attack points for each control you were visiting? e.g. a corner in a road makes a good attack point. (for the non-orienteers, an attack point is a clear, easy to find feature handy to your control – you navigate carefully from your attack point). E.g. for 101, the nearest good attack point is the saddle that the road passes through to the south west of point 1019. For 46, the top of the hill 839m is your attack point, for control 60, the fence junction, and so on.
  • Did you look for catching features for your controls? Something you will easily recognise that tells you that you have gone past your control site. Line features like creeks, fences and roads make good catching features. For control 101, the flat topped spur running east/west from point 1019 is a very helpful catching feature (or attack point if you were approaching from 56)
  • Did you count contours between controls? At least one team climbed from 82 up to 46, a climb of 360 vertical metres. Was it worth the effort?
  • Did you weigh up route choices between controls, looking for the most efficient order for groups of controls?
  • Did you measure the straight line distances between the controls on your planned route? Did you work out roughly where you were likely to be when it got dark, and therefore which controls you would be trying to find in the dark?
  • Unless you are experienced navigators on a 1:50,000 map in totally unfamiliar terrain in the dark, you should have been aiming to look for EASY controls during darkness. Easy  = good attack points, close to the control, or controls on easy to identify features: 70, 12, 24, 46, 74, 55, 65 etc.
  • As is required for orienteering, every control was on a feature marked on the map: if the control description said “gully” or “ spur” then that gully or spur was big enough to be marked on the map. The smaller folds in the land don’t appear on the map contour lines because the 1:50,000 scale is too small.
  • Did you consider the option of coming back to the event centre during the night for food, to read the map and to review your plan in good lighting? The winning team spent about half an hour at the woolshed having soup and cheese toasties and planning their “second half”. About half of all the competitors came back during the night, some only for food, some for food and sleep.
  • Do you see why we gave you 3 hours to plan? Did you make the most of that time, while you were fresh, the map was easy to read (good light, no sweat in the eyes or rain on the plastic bag)?

We heard lots of comments about teams not being able to find controls even though they were sure that THEY were in the right place, and they looked for “hours”, so we heard. If you think you are in the right place/on the right feature, and about the right altitude, and you spend more than a few minutes looking for a control and can’t find it, then YOU are NOT in the right place!!!! You need to relocate (another orienteering term) yourself back to a recognisable feature and try again. Or give up on that control. A rogaine is about navigating using a map, it is NOT a treasure hunt where you cast about the countryside hunting for control flags, and if you are lucky you find one!!!  Interestingly, for every team that swore the control flag was “not there”, or “too hard”, there was another team who said, “we had no trouble finding it”.
