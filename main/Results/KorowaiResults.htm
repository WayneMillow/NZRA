Thoughts from the Organiser, Grant Hunter

A great setting on a gem of a day - fine and clear, a touch of snow from a fall earlier in the week on the background peaks, Lakes Lyndon and Coleridge as blue as blue, and a lightly cooling breeze. That’s about as good as it gets. It was a good place to be, whatever the calling. ‘Heights of Korowai’ rogaine seemed to fit in okay.

Congratulations to the winning team of Chris Forne, Phil Novis and Ed Rimmer with 1510 out of a possible 1570. A perhaps even more remarkable performance was that of father and son team Desmond and Duncan O’Regan with 1470, 90 points clear of third place-getters Julian MacLaren and Michael Smithson. My congratulations not only to these teams but all of you. (We’ll try to get the winner’s route choice up on the web for you.)

With the winning team missing just two low-score checkpoints, the extent of the course relative to the duration was about right. Dave Laurie did a great job allocating the point value to each control, ensuring teams started in all possible directions. This also shows up in the even-ness of control visits (see graph).

I’ve explained already why some controls were hard to find. The high country landscape is large in scale and open, making navigation relatively easy. If you paused at many places you could see almost exactly where several other controls were located. To compensate I placed some flags close to the ground, the alternative being that they could be seen from far away. Some teams spent many minutes searching in the vicinity, which was NOT my intention, and I regret not getting the degree of visibility quite right. I’ll come clean and say that about 11 am, when we learned of these difficulties, we lifted flags 16, 24, 31, 54 and 43 a little to improve their visibility, thus upsetting the ‘level playing field’ across all teams, but maybe reducing frustration for some. (Hence you should take some relative scores with a grain of salt.)

When bringing in the controls I rechecked one or two that some teams had found problematical. For example, I stand by my description for 32 - SW end of a low faultline ridge. Even Dave Laurie failed to find that one at control-collection time.

I’ve said before that organising rogaines, too, can be an adventure. New terrain and facilities each time, uncertain timing and clumping of teams finishing, and vagaries of the weather etc. requires a flexible approach. Counting results and getting food out before teams head home is always a crunch point. This time I discovered the bags of tea, coffee and milo untouched after teams had departed and I imagine the Springfield café profited from that slip. We owe you a cuppa sometime. I thank Bill Kennedy and Dave Laurie for their roles leading towards the event, Tom, Jude, Richie, Anne and Bayne for their help on Saturday, and the backup team from Pegasus SAR.

Keep an eye on www.rogaine.org.nz for other events, but if you’re new to map sports think about orienteering – a shorter, sharper event with a funny-coloured map. Peninsula and Plains Orienteers runs events close to Christchurch most fortnights so check them out at www.geocities.com/paponz .

If you’re more inclined to tramping, the Torlesse Korowai Park is much larger than the bit you visited on Saturday. It extends over a surprisingly large area, the full length of the Torlesse Range right through to the Benmore Range and our event area west of Lake Lyndon. Email me if you want a descriptive brochure and map of the area or pick one up from a DOC office. See you next time.

Grant Hunter


Pete Squires Enthuses

This was a real Southern Man event; not for the faint-hearted or unacclimatised Aussies. Hills, steep hills, and lots of them, covered with scree and scrub of all persuasions.

Yet in the middle of all this were some beautiful patches of beech forest, an abundance of sparkling streams with water of thirst-quenching freshness, and some views that stopped even the most ardent competitors in their tracks. The stunning blue of Lake Coleridge 1000 metres below, garnished with sun sparkle and whitecaps, and set in a distant backdrop of snow-covered Alps holding back angry stormclouds makes a unique view. It is so close to our back door yet how often do we see it?

And in spite of the hills, there were some good stretches of enjoyable yet challenging flat country, both plains and alpine streams, nestled in the valleys which helped create the hills.

A 9 am to 4 pm event in mid-summer is an ideal days outing, long enough to want to stop but short enough to ignore the blisters. The 34 checkpoints appeared close on a 14 cm by 12 cm area of a 1:50,000 map, and this gave plenty of interest and plenty of route choice. Right from the start, the 45 teams headed off in many different directions; always a good indication of a well-planned course.

There were the usual perpetual runners, some dedicated hill-walkers, and a good collection of people of more sensible persuasion who had come to enjoy a solid walk in some unique South Island high country. Everyone enjoyed the beautiful day, the spectacular country, and the strategic challenge of such a good course. Grant is to be congratulated for finding such variety in the high country.

Climate-wise, this course could only be used in summer. Even then, there was snow lying on the course two nights before the event, and the road over Porter’s Pass was closed on Thursday morning.

I could bore you with What We Did and Why, or What Someone Else Did and Why Not, but I won’t. The results explain it all. Suffice to say that the Oldies are getting older and the Young’uns are getting faster!

In spite of its name, no glide time was allowed in this immaculately managed rogaine. It will probably be the forerunner of an annual summer event, judging by its success. Grant Hunter and his able crew have put in a tremendous effort to bring this event to fruition, and they thoroughly deserved the heartfelt thanks led by Trish Faulkner after the prizegiving.

Pete Squires.
